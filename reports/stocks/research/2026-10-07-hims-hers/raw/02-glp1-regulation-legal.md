## 2. Compounding law from first principles

### 2.1 What compounding is
Compounding means a pharmacy mixes a drug itself instead of selling a factory-made, FDA-approved product. Compounded drugs are not FDA-approved. FDA does not review them for safety or effectiveness before sale (background law).

The Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act (FD&C Act) gives two legal routes (background law).

### 2.2 Section 503A: traditional pharmacies
- Regulated mainly by state boards of pharmacy.
- Must compound for an **identified individual patient**, based on a prescription.
- Can use a bulk ingredient only if it has a USP monograph, is part of an FDA-approved drug, or is on the 503A bulks list. Semaglutide and tirzepatide are parts of approved drugs, so 503A pharmacies can use them.
- Must not compound "regularly or in inordinate amounts" drugs that are **"essentially copies"** of a commercially available drug.
(background law; 21 U.S.C. 353a)

### 2.3 Section 503B: outsourcing facilities
- Register with FDA. Follow full factory-quality rules (CGMP). FDA inspects them.
- Can make large batches **without** a patient-specific prescription and ship to clinics.
- Can use a bulk ingredient only if (a) it is on the **503B bulks list** (a list of ingredients with a "clinical need"), or (b) the drug is on FDA's **drug shortage list**.
- Also barred from making "essentially a copy" of an approved drug.
(background law; 21 U.S.C. 353b)

### 2.4 The "essentially a copy" rule
- A compounded drug is "essentially a copy" if it has the same active ingredient, strength and route as an approved drug that is commercially available.
- A change (different dose, added vitamin B12, different form) only escapes this if the **prescriber** decides the change makes a **significant clinical difference for that patient**, and records it.
- FDA's long-standing guidance says the prescriber's determination must be specific to the patient. A marketing claim of "personalization" applied to a mass customer base does not meet that test in FDA's view.
(background law; FDA 503A/503B "essentially a copy" guidance, Jan 2018)

### 2.5 The shortage exemption
- While a drug is on FDA's shortage list, it is treated as not "commercially available". So the copy limit does not bite. 503B facilities may also compound it from bulk.
- This is why compounded semaglutide was legal at scale in 2022-2024.
- Semaglutide shortage ended 2025-02-21. Grace periods ended 2025-04-22 (503A) and 2025-05-22 (503B). (healio.com; mwe.com)
- After that, compounded semaglutide is legal only as a 503A product that is not "essentially a copy", meaning a patient-specific clinical reason for the difference.

### 2.6 "Personalization": the Hims position
- Hims' legal footing since 2025 is that its doctors prescribe individualized doses, which they say makes each product not a copy. (pharmaceutical-technology.com)
- Novo's view (June 2025): this is "mass sales of compounded drugs under the false guise of 'personalization'" (allegation). (beckershospitalreview.com)
- FDA's view (Sept 2025 letter): Hims could not say compounded drugs have "the same active ingredient" as Wegovy/Ozempic. (biospace.com)
- No court has ruled on whether Hims' personalization meets the law. Novo's patent case was dropped before any ruling.

### 3.2 How Hims earns money on branded drugs
- Under the 2025 Novo deal and the 2026 Lilly access, prescriptions are filled by the **maker's own pharmacy** (NovoCare, LillyDirect). (beckershospitalreview.com; fiercehealthcare.com)
- Under the March 2026 Novo settlement, Hims sells at **Novo's self-pay prices**. (statnews.com; cnbc.com)
- So Hims cannot mark up the drug. It earns a membership or consult fee on top, and maybe a fee from the maker. No maker fee was disclosed in sources found.
- Whether Hims books the drug price as revenue (gross) or only its fee (net) was not found. This matters for revenue growth versus margin. See gaps.
- Q2 2026 gross margin fell to 64% from 76%. A summary of the call ties part of this to "investments in branded weight-loss products". (yahoo finance call summary; qz.com)

### 3.3 Why the price deals squeeze Hims
- The maker now sells the same drug to the same patient direct at $149-$350. Hims cannot undercut that.
- Hims' extra charge must be justified by service (doctor access, coaching, add-ons). A $149/month membership on top of a $149 pill doubles the patient's cost. Patients can go to LillyDirect, NovoCare or TrumpRx instead.
- Medicare patients will pay $50 through Medicare. Hims is mostly cash-pay, so it is unlikely to win that group.
- Old compounded semaglutide was sold at roughly $199-$299 a month with Hims making the drug, so Hims kept most of the price. That margin pool is mostly gone.

### 3.4 Is Hims just a distribution channel?
My read (analyst judgment, not a source fact): on branded GLP-1s, yes, largely. Hims is a customer-acquisition and prescribing front end for Novo and Lilly. It earns a service fee, not a drug margin. Its pricing power depends on brand and convenience, not on the drug. The maker sets the drug price and can pull access, as Novo showed in June 2025. Lilly explicitly is not a partner. Upside depends on (a) membership retention, (b) add-on products (labs, testosterone, other peptides), and (c) any undisclosed maker fees.

### 4.5 DEA telehealth prescribing (controlled substances)
- The Ryan Haight Act (2008) normally requires an in-person visit before a controlled substance is prescribed online.

- Hims relevance: testosterone is Schedule III. If Hims prescribes testosterone (or any controlled drug) without in-person visits, the end-2026 cliff matters. I did not confirm the size of Hims' testosterone business or any ADHD offering. A vendor blog says questionnaire-only intake is not enough for testosterone (unverified).

## (a) 10-bullet summary

1. Hims' GLP-1 business has moved from "make it ourselves" (compounded semaglutide, launched 2024) to "sell the maker's drug" (branded Wegovy, Ozempic, Zepbound) after the 9 Mar 2026 Novo settlement.
2. The legal basis for mass compounding ended when FDA declared the semaglutide shortage over on 21 Feb 2025 (grace periods to 22 Apr / 22 May 2025).
3. Hims then relied on "personalized" 503A compounding. Novo, FDA and HHS all attacked that. No court ever ruled on it.
4. Feb 2026 was the turning point. Hims launched a $49 compounded pill on 5 Feb. FDA named Hims and HHS referred it to DOJ on 6 Feb. Hims pulled the pill on 7 Feb. Novo sued on 9 Feb.
5. The Novo settlement dropped the patent suit without prejudice. Hims stopped advertising compounded GLP-1s. No money terms were found.
6. FDA's 503B bulks exclusion for semaglutide, tirzepatide and liraglutide is still **proposed** (30 Apr / 1 May 2026). It barely affects Hims directly, because Hims uses 503A pharmacies for GLP-1s.
7. On branded drugs, the maker sets the price ($149-$350 cash after the Nov 2025 Trump deal). Hims earns a service or membership fee. It is mostly a distribution channel there. Gross margin fell from 76% to 64% in Q2 2026.
8. The biggest live legal risk is now **FTC v. Hims** (filed 29 Jul 2026, with Utah and LA County): alleged health-data sharing with Meta/Snap and alleged subscription billing violations. Hims has accrued an amount but warns it could be "materially higher".
9. Two securities class actions (2025 Novo-related; 2026 FTC-related, lead plaintiff deadline 2 Nov 2026) and a consumer privacy class action are pending.
10. Dated catalysts: 2 Nov 2026 lead plaintiff deadline; Q3 results (early Nov, est.); FDA DTC ad proposed rule target Dec 2026; DEA telehealth waiver expiry 31 Dec 2026; open-dated 503B final decision and DOJ outcome.
