## (b) How to use sell-side ratings — a first-principles note

- **Conflicts of interest.** Banks earn fees from the companies they cover: underwriting, M&A, lending. HIMS is an example: JPMorgan arranged its $400m facility and Goldman was a lender. A Buy can help keep the client. The 2003 US Global Research Analyst Settlement split research from banking for this reason. *(Background knowledge; not re-verified in this run.)*
- **Skew toward Buy.** Most published ratings are Buy or Hold. True Sells are rare. In this file, only HSBC on LLY (Reduce) and BofA on Amrize (Underperform) are explicit negatives. A "Neutral" from a big bank is often the polite form of a negative view. Note that NVO's slide came through Neutral cuts, not Sells.
- **Target drift toward price.** Targets tend to follow the share price. Analysts raise them after rallies and cut them after falls. Examples here: BofA on HIMS moved $32 → $30 → $25 → $36 → $37 within months. JPM on TSLA went $145 → $475 → $445 → $415. A target is closer to a lagging summary of the price than a forecast.
- **Predictive evidence (snippets, unverified):**
  - Bradshaw & Brown (2005), ~100k US targets, 1997–2002: price met or beat the target at 12 months only ~26% of the time, and touched it at some point in the year only ~35% (via CXO Advisory summary).
  - Later international studies find higher hit rates within 12 months: ~63% (India, Kadam & Sethi 2024), ~67% (Poland), ~52% (Denmark, 2010–2020 thesis). Optimistic targets are hit less often.
  - A US study using Capital IQ consensus targets (2004–2025) finds mean absolute error of ~41 points for Buy targets versus ~32 for Sells. The Sell sample is small.
  - A Polish study found ratings still carry short-run directional information even when the target is missed.
  - *(Background knowledge, not re-verified here:)* Womack (1996) found rating changes, especially downgrades, move prices and drift for weeks. Barber et al. (2001) found the returns from following consensus mostly vanish after trading costs.
- **Practical use.**
  1. Treat **changes** (upgrades/downgrades, list adds/drops) as the signal, not rating levels.
  2. Weight downgrades and rare Sells more than Buys.
  3. Read the reason and the model inputs, e.g. MS's "$60/share for Optimus at 50% odds". The input is more useful than the target.
  4. Compare a target to the current price only if the target is recent.
  5. Watch for a big gap between target and price that is not shrinking (HSAI). It can mean the targets are stale.
