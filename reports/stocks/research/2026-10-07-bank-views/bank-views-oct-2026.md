# What the Big Banks Think About Stocks (October 2026)

*Research date 7 October 2026. Five research streams: strategist targets for ten banks, flagship pick lists and single-stock ratings, a Goldman Sachs deep dive, a JPMorgan deep dive, and Morgan Stanley, Bank of America, UBS and Citi themes. Raw reports with sources are in `raw/`. Every bank document was blocked from this sandbox. ## 1. The answer first

1. **The banks are bullish, but little room is left this year.** Nine of ten big banks (one, UBS, through its wealth arm) expect the S&P 500 to end 2026 between 7,950 and 8,100. The median is 8,000. The index closed at a record 7,818.93 on 6 October (sources differ slightly), only 2.3% below that median. Many of these targets are months old. Only two of the nine year-end targets, Barclays' and HSBC's, date from September. Goldman's is from May (its 12-month call is from mid-September; unverified), Citi's and Wells Fargo's are from about June, and Deutsche Bank's is from late 2025 (it raised its earnings forecasts in August). We found no October target change at any bank. Bank of America is the lone bear at 7,400 (mid-September); its strategist said then that stocks were overdue for a pullback (see section 5). The banks' 12-month and 2027 targets (7,800 to 8,800, some unverified) imply about 0% to +12.5% from here.
2. **The bull case rests on earnings growth, and AI drives a large share of it.** Every 2026 target raise we found came from higher earnings forecasts, not higher multiples. Goldman says AI drives about half of 2026 S&P earnings growth. The banks with figures expect earnings growth to slow from about 23–36% in 2026 to about 11–17% in 2027.
3. **No bank calls AI a bubble outright, but the worry has moved to how it is paid for.** (Hartnett write-ups talk of policymakers trying to "keep the bubble alive"; secondary, unverified.) JPMorgan expects $4.1 trillion of AI spending to be debt-funded by 2030 (June). Fund managers in Bank of America's September survey named a disorderly rise in bond yields as the top risk. 42% picked hyperscaler capex as the likeliest source of a systemic credit event. Hyperscalers are the biggest cloud computing companies, and capex is their capital spending on data centres and equipment. A systemic credit event is a wave of debt defaults that spreads through the financial system.
4. **The real split is on interest rates.** The Fed raised rates on 16 September 2026 for the first time since 2023. Bank of America expects two more hikes this year. Citi expects a pause and a cut in June 2027. Both calls came before the weak 2 October jobs report (secondary reports; unverified), which cut the odds of an October hike. Morgan Stanley's Mike Wilson named a 10-year yield above 5% as the biggest risk. The 10-year yield is the interest rate on 10-year US government bonds. Wilson said a move above 5% would turn consolidation (prices moving sideways) into a correction (a sharper fall). Secondary reports say the 10-year passed 5% in mid-September and reached about 5.2–5.3% in late September, its highest since about 2007. On 1 October it briefly rose above its 2007 peak, to about 5.3%, its highest since 2002. It eased to about 5.2% on 2 October, touched about 5.3% again on 5 October and was about 5.26% on 6 October (all unverified; levels vary by source; check a live quote). On those figures his trigger has already been crossed. Even so, Wilson's latest comments (about 2 October; unverified) put drawdown risk at 5–10% and he planned to add risk-on names in October, and the index closed at a record on 6 October.
5. **Over ten years, the three banks with forecasts we found expect weak US returns.** Goldman (Nov 2025) expects about 6.5% a year from the S&P 500, below Europe (7.1%) and emerging markets (10.9%). J.P. Morgan Asset Management's 2026 assumptions (Oct 2025) give US large caps 6.7% a year over 10–15 years, below Europe (8.5%) and emerging markets (7.2%) (both unverified). Bank of America's valuation model implies about −3% a year over 10 years for the cap-weighted index (the standard S&P 500, which weights each company by its stock-market value, so the biggest count most). We did not find the date of this figure. The Goldman and JPMorgan figures are about a year old; JPMorgan's 2027 edition is due soon.

The implied P/E is our arithmetic: year-end target divided by 2027 earnings per share (EPS), except HSBC's and UBS's own figures. UBS's target comes from its wealth CIO office, not the investment bank (Reuters, 21 Aug).

**How a target is built.** Think of it as a recipe with two ingredients. Price = next year's earnings per share × a multiple. JPMorgan's $420 of 2027 EPS × 19.0 gives about 8,000. The earnings part is a forecast. The multiple is a judgement. On $400 of EPS, one point of multiple is worth 400 index points, about 5%. About 1.5 points of multiple (600 index points) is the whole gap between Bank of America's 7,400 and the 8,000 median.

**Why the same number can hide different views, or different dates.** Goldman, JPMorgan, Morgan Stanley and Deutsche Bank all sit at 8,000. In May, Goldman's 2027 EPS was $385. JPMorgan and Deutsche raised theirs to $420 in August, after strong results, from $390 each. On our arithmetic (8,000 ÷ $385 = 20.8x), Goldman's May figures meant a higher multiple on lower earnings. But secondary reports say Goldman's September note raised 2027 EPS to $415 (unverified). On that figure the gap to JPMorgan and Deutsche is $5, not $35, and 8,000 ÷ $415 = 19.3x, close to their 19.0x (our arithmetic). So most of the gap was timing. Goldman itself said in May the multiple would stay "close to the current 21". Its September note put the market's forward P/E at about 19x, near the 10-year average. These two Goldman figures are four months apart, and our sources do not say which earnings each uses.

**How much to trust these targets.** Not much, by the record. Two sources that measure from 2000 found that year-end strategist targets missed by about 13–15 percentage points a year on average (Motley Fool using Birinyi data; Jeff Sommer in the NYT). In the Motley Fool study, a naive rule of "+9% every year" did about as well. That study is from February 2015, so it covers no year after 2014, and the Sommer figure is undated in our sources. A separate IFA study of 2018–23 found misses of about 18 percentage points. All of these are snippet-level. The reasons are structural:
- Multiples swing more than earnings, and nobody forecasts them well.
- Career risk rewards staying near the pack. This year nine of ten sit in a 150-point band.
- The consensus has not forecast a down year since 2000 (Bespoke, unverified), so it misses bear markets.
- In bull markets they chase the index. JPMorgan changed its target four times in 2026: a cut to 7,200, then raises to 7,600, 7,800 and 8,000.

## 3. What they agree on

- **Earnings, not multiples, drove the 2026 target raises we found.** For second-quarter 2026 results, 80–86% of reporting companies beat analysts' earnings forecasts, against a long-run average of about 67–73%. Underlying earnings growth was about 35%.
- **AI capex is the engine.** Barclays expects hyperscaler capital spending above $1.1 trillion in 2027. Goldman's equity strategists expect hyperscaler capex of about $800bn in 2026, $1.2 trillion in 2027 and $1.4 trillion in 2028 (secondary sources; unverified); a Goldman economics note implies about $1.1 trillion for 2027 (different scope; date not found; unverified). JPMorgan Global Research expects $5.5 trillion of global AI-related capex through 2030 (June), up from $5.1 trillion.
- **Earnings growth slows in 2027.** Every bank with figures expects slower earnings growth in 2027 than in 2026 (see section 1). Goldman ties part of this to AI: it says AI capex added about 11 percentage points to 2026 EPS growth, falling to about 7 in 2027 and to zero or below in 2028 as depreciation catches up (unverified, aggregator). Depreciation is the cost of the equipment spread over its life. Once spending stops growing fast, that cost catches up with the revenue boost.
- **Power is a bottleneck.** Goldman, JPMorgan and Morgan Stanley all name power supply as a constraint on the AI build-out. Morgan Stanley goes further and says power access, not money, is the binding constraint (20 August). JPMorgan says cash flow covers the spend for now, but the risk is the shift to debt funding (June; see section 1). Goldman sees global data-centre power demand 220% higher in 2030 than in 2023 (March). JPMorgan expects $5.8 trillion of global grid spending in the decade to 2035 (report date unclear, about early 2026). Morgan Stanley estimated in August a US data-centre power shortfall of about 38 GW (gigawatts) through 2028 (later secondary write-ups say 32 GW or 57 GW; conflicting and unverified).
- **Inflation, the Fed and bond yields are the shared top risk.** Bank of America, Barclays, Wells Fargo, Morgan Stanley and HSBC all name some version of it (HSBC flags inflation prints but calls Fed-hike fears "overdone").
- **The rally is narrow.** Fewer than half of S&P 500 stocks trade above their 200-day average (Wilson, in a summary of about 5 October; unverified), even though the index closed at a record on 6 October. A stock's 200-day average is its mean price over the last 200 trading days. Bank of America prefers the equal-weight index, which gives every stock the same weight, to the cap-weighted one. J.P. Morgan Asset Management's 4Q 2026 guide puts AI-related industries at about 53% of S&P 500 market value and the seven biggest tech companies (the "Magnificent 7") at about 35% (secondary summary; unverified). In an older figure, Goldman put the top 10 stocks at 41% of market value but 34% of earnings.

## 5. The warnings worth hearing

- **Bank of America's fund-manager survey (4–10 September).** Cash rose to 3.9% but stayed under 4%. Under Bank of America's "cash rule", a contrarian sentiment gauge, fund-manager cash below 4% is read as a sell signal for stocks, so that signal stays on. 53% of managers named owning global chip stocks ("long semiconductors") as the most crowded trade, down from a record 82% in July. The 53% matches August's figure, so the two surveys may have been mixed up (unverified). All the survey figures come from secondary write-ups.
- **Michael Hartnett (Bank of America).** His Bull & Bear indicator was 9.7 in August and about 9.5–9.6 in September, in sell territory (above 8). A 3 October summary says stay risk-off until the dollar peaks, and start adding bonds. He sees a Democratic midterm sweep on 3 November as a risk of a 10%+ selloff. All of this comes from secondary write-ups.
- **Savita Subramanian (Bank of America).** The Street's lone bear said in mid-September that stocks were overdue for a pullback (target note dated 14 or 18 September by different sources; Bloomberg video, 17 September). She noted only one 5% dip this year, in March, against about three in a normal year. She said about half of her bear-market indicators were triggered, and that P/Es at the time implied inflation well below Bank of America's own forecast.
- **Mike Wilson (Morgan Stanley).** He warned in mid-September of a correction within 30 days if oil reached $120–140, and later put drawdown risk at 5–10% tied to rising yields (unverified).
- **Jamie Dimon (JPMorgan).** He has been the house sceptic all year. He compared markets to 2005–07 in February, saw "a little bit too much exuberance" in May, and said "you may have seen peak private credit" in July (wording unverified). In early October he reportedly said AI spending now competes with government borrowing for capital and that rates are going up, but that he still thinks AI will pay off (unverified; one fragmentary source).
- **UBS's wealth CIO is the counterweight: the most relaxed of Morgan Stanley, Bank of America, UBS and Citi.** Mark Haefele (17 September) says AI spending is paying off and the rally can run another 6–12 months.

## 8. How to use bank research

- **Watch the changes, not the levels.** Upgrades, downgrades and list additions carry information. A Buy rating on its own carries little. Most published ratings are Buy or Hold, and a Neutral from a big bank is often a polite negative.
- **Targets move a lot.** Bank of America's Hims target has been reported at $21, $25, $30, $32, $36 and $37 within months (order not confirmed). JPMorgan's Tesla target went $145 → $475 → $445 → $415. Targets tend to follow the share price, so a target is often closer to a lagging summary of the price than a forecast. Our sources do not show the share prices behind these two examples. They give reasons for some of the moves: a Q1 miss, Q2 sales trends and an FDA peptide review for Hims, and a humanoid-robot thesis for Tesla.
- **The record is weak.** In one large US study (Bradshaw and Brown, 2005, about 100,000 targets from 1997–2002), the price met or beat the 12-month target at the deadline only about 26% of the time. It touched the target at some point in the year only about 35% of the time. Later studies outside the US found higher hit rates within 12 months: about 63% in India (Kadam and Sethi, 2024), about 67% in Poland and about 52% in Denmark (a 2010–2020 thesis). Optimistic targets were hit less often. A newer US study of consensus targets (2004–2025) found that Buy targets missed by about 41 points on average and Sell targets by about 32 (small Sell sample). All of these are snippets and unverified.
- **Read the inputs.** Morgan Stanley reportedly values Optimus at $60 a share, weighted at 50% odds (aggregator snippet; unverified). That input is more useful than its Tesla target, because you can disagree with a specific number.
- **Remember the conflicts.** Banks earn fees from the companies they cover. JPMorgan arranged Hims' $400m receivables facility (a working-capital line signed 1 July 2026 that turns money owed to Hims into cash), and Goldman is reported as a lender in it (aggregator; unverified). JPMorgan also rates Hims Overweight (May; unverified; see section 7).
