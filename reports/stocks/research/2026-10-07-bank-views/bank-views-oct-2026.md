*Research date 7 October 2026. ## 1. The answer first

1. **The banks are bullish, but little room is left this year.** Nine of ten big banks (one, UBS, through its wealth arm) expect the S&P 500 to end 2026 between 7,950 and 8,100. The median is 8,000. The index closed at a record 7,818.93 on 6 October (sources differ slightly), only 2.3% below that median. Bank of America is the lone bear at 7,400. Their 12-month and 2027 targets (7,800 to 8,800, some unverified) imply about 0% to +12.5% from here.
2. **The bull case is all earnings, and most of it is AI.** Every 2026 target raise we found came from higher earnings forecasts, not higher multiples. Goldman says AI drives about half of 2026 S&P earnings growth. The banks with figures expect earnings growth to slow from about 23–35% in 2026 to about 12–17% in 2027.
3. **No bank calls AI a bubble outright, but the worry has moved to how it is paid for.** (Hartnett write-ups talk of policymakers trying to "keep the bubble alive"; secondary, unverified.) JPMorgan expects $4.1 trillion of AI spending to be debt-funded by 2030 (June). Fund managers in Bank of America's September survey named a disorderly rise in bond yields as the top risk. 42% picked hyperscaler capex as the likeliest source of a credit event.
4. **The real split is on interest rates.** The Fed raised rates on 16 September 2026 for the first time since 2023. Bank of America expects two more hikes this year. Citi expects a pause and a cut in June 2027. Morgan Stanley's Mike Wilson named a 10-year yield above 5% as the biggest risk, one that would turn consolidation into a correction. Secondary reports say the 10-year passed 5% in mid-September and reached about 5.2–5.3% in late September, its highest since about 2007, then eased to about 5.2% on 2 October (unverified; check a live quote). On those figures his trigger has already been crossed. Even so, Wilson's latest comments (about 2 October; unverified) put drawdown risk at 5–10% and he planned to add risk-on names in October, and the index closed at a record on 6 October.
5. **Over ten years, the three banks with forecasts we found expect weak US returns.** Goldman (Nov 2025) expects about 6.5% a year from the S&P 500, below Europe (7.1%) and emerging markets (10.9%). J.P. Morgan Asset Management's 2026 assumptions (Oct 2025) give US large caps 6.7% a year over 10–15 years, below Europe (8.5%) and emerging markets (7.2%) (both unverified). Bank of America's valuation model implies about −3% a year for the cap-weighted index. The Goldman and JPMorgan figures are about a year old; JPMorgan's 2027 edition is due soon.

The implied P/E is our arithmetic: year-end target divided by 2027 earnings per share (EPS), except HSBC's and UBS's own figures. UBS's target comes from its wealth CIO office, not the investment bank (Reuters, 21 Aug).

**How a target is built.** Think of it as a recipe with two ingredients. Price = next year's earnings per share × a multiple. JPMorgan's $420 of 2027 EPS × 19.0 gives about 8,000. The earnings part is a forecast. The multiple is a judgement. On $400 of EPS, one point of multiple is worth 400 index points, about 5%. About 1.5 points of multiple (600 index points) is the whole gap between Bank of America's 7,400 and the 8,000 median.

**Why the same number hides different views.** Goldman, JPMorgan, Morgan Stanley and Deutsche Bank all sit at 8,000. But Goldman's 2027 EPS is $385 and JPMorgan's and Deutsche's is $420, a $35 gap. So, on our arithmetic (8,000 ÷ $385 = 20.8x), Goldman is paying a higher multiple for lower earnings. Goldman itself said in May the multiple would stay "close to the current 21". Its September note put the market's forward P/E at about 19x, near the 10-year average. These two Goldman figures are four months apart, and our sources do not say which earnings each uses.

**How much to trust these targets.** Not much, by the record. Since 2000, year-end strategist targets have missed by about 13–15 percentage points a year on average (about 18 in 2018–23). A naive rule of "+9% every year" did about as well (Motley Fool using Birinyi data; Jeff Sommer in the NYT; IFA for 2018–23; all snippet-level). The reasons are structural:
- Multiples swing more than earnings, and nobody forecasts them well.
- Career risk rewards staying near the pack. This year nine of ten sit in a 150-point band.
- The consensus has not forecast a down year since 2000 (Bespoke, unverified), so it misses bear markets.
- In bull markets they chase the index. JPMorgan changed its target four times in 2026: a cut to 7,200, then raises to 7,600, 7,800 and 8,000.

## 3. What they agree on

- **Earnings, not multiples, drive the market.** Second-quarter 2026 beat rates were 80–86%, against a long-run average of about 67–73%. Underlying earnings growth was about 35%.
- **AI capex is the engine.** Barclays expects hyperscaler capital spending above $1.1 trillion in 2027. Goldman's equity strategists expect hyperscaler capex of about $800bn in 2026, $1.2 trillion in 2027 and $1.4 trillion in 2028 (secondary sources; unverified); a Goldman economics note implies about $1.1 trillion for 2027. JPMorgan Global Research expects $5.5 trillion of global AI-related capex through 2030 (June), up from $5.1 trillion.
- **The AI tailwind fades.** Goldman says AI capex added about 11 points to 2026 EPS growth, falling to about 7 in 2027 and to zero or below in 2028 as depreciation catches up (unverified, aggregator). Depreciation is the cost of the equipment spread over its life. Once spending stops growing fast, that cost catches up with the revenue boost.
- **Power is the bottleneck, not money.** Goldman sees global data-centre power demand 220% higher in 2030 than in 2023 (March). JPMorgan expects $5.8 trillion of global grid spending in the decade to 2035 (report date unclear, about early 2026). Morgan Stanley estimates a US data-centre power gap of about 38 GW through 2028 (later write-ups say 32 GW or 57 GW; conflicting).
- **Inflation, the Fed and bond yields are the shared top risk.** Bank of America, Barclays, Wells Fargo, Morgan Stanley and HSBC all name some version of it (HSBC flags inflation prints but calls Fed-hike fears "overdone").
- **The rally is narrow.** Fewer than half of S&P 500 stocks trade above their 200-day average (Wilson, unverified). Bank of America prefers the equal-weight index to the cap-weighted one.

## 5. The warnings worth hearing

- **Bank of America's fund-manager survey (4–10 September).** Cash rose to 3.9% but stayed under 4%, so its "cash rule" sell signal stays on. The most crowded trade was long semiconductors at 53%, down from a record 82% in July. The 53% matches August's figure, so the two surveys may have been mixed up (unverified).
- **Michael Hartnett (Bank of America).** His Bull & Bear indicator was 9.7 in August and about 9.5–9.6 in September, in sell territory (above 8). A 3 October summary says stay risk-off until the dollar peaks, and start adding bonds. He sees a Democratic midterm sweep on 3 November as a risk of a 10%+ selloff. All of this comes from secondary write-ups.
- **Mike Wilson (Morgan Stanley).** He warned in mid-September of a correction within 30 days if oil reached $120–140, and later put drawdown risk at 5–10% tied to rising yields (unverified).
- **Jamie Dimon (JPMorgan).** He has been the house sceptic all year. He compared markets to 2005–07 in February, saw "too much exuberance" in May, and said "you may have seen peak private credit" in July (wording unverified). In early October he reportedly said AI spending now competes with government borrowing for capital (unverified).
- **UBS's wealth CIO is the main counterweight.** Mark Haefele (17 September) says AI spending is paying off and the rally can run another 6–12 months.

## 8. How to use bank research

- **Watch the changes, not the levels.** Upgrades, downgrades and list additions carry information. A Buy rating on its own carries little. Most published ratings are Buy or Hold, and a Neutral from a big bank is often a polite negative.
- **Targets chase prices.** Bank of America's Hims target has been reported at $21, $25, $30, $32, $36 and $37 within months (order not confirmed). JPMorgan's Tesla target went $145 → $475 → $445 → $415. A target is closer to a lagging summary of the price than a forecast.
- **The record is weak.** In one large US study (Bradshaw and Brown, 2005, about 100,000 targets), the price met the 12-month target at the deadline only about 26% of the time (snippet-level).
- **Read the inputs.** Morgan Stanley's "$60 a share for Optimus at 50% odds" is more useful than its $415 Tesla target, because you can disagree with a specific number.
- **Remember the conflicts.** Banks earn fees from the companies they cover. JPMorgan arranged Hims' $400m working-capital facility, and Goldman is a lender to it.
